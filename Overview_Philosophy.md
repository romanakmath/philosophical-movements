## 1. Nihilism
Nihilism, which was profoundly shaped by Friedrich W. Nietzsche (1844-1900), is a well-known philosophical concept in modern times.
The main belief is the rise of modern science leads to a decline of religious frameworks,
and challenges the perspective of whether absolute "true" or "false" values exist.
Instead nihilism argues that any objective structure we perceive is the one we have constructed ourselves.  
Consequently, nihilism rejects religions and other institutions that claim a monopoly on truth. Nietzsche´s philosophy urges 
individuals to live within their own boundaries and lived experiences to actively create their own reality and meaning.

### Origin & Historical Context
The earliest roots of nihilism can be traced back to the late Middle Ages (14th century) and the nominalism developed by Wilhelm of Ockham. Here, Ockham argues that God is not bound by human reason or objective moral truths. Instead, he conceived of God as an omnipotent, completely free, and unpredictable will, breaking with the medieval synthesis of faith and reason.

In the late 17th century, Ockham´s nominalism shifted through the philosophy of René Descartes and his scientific method of *mathesis universalis*.
Confronted with an unpredictable universe, and loss of reliance on a pre-existing order, Michael A. Gillespie argues 
that the idea of an absolute will gradually shifted toward humanity, as humans began to master and structure the world by their own will and reason.

Later, in the 18th century (beginning in 1783), sparked by a dispute with Moses Mendelssohn over Gotthold E. Lessing and his adherence to Spinozism, 
Friedrich H. Jacobi launched a major philosophical attack against the rationalist Enlightenment concept by Lessing. While Mendelssohn defended natural theology and offered different readings of Lessing´s Spinoza, Jacobi argued that rationalism and deterministic pantheism dissolve human freedom. In the broader debates of the era, Kant insisted on his postulates of practical reason, Fichte questioned the worth of "heteronomous" Christian ethics, Hegel set up a renewed different kind of idealist rationalism and Herder, Goethe and Schelling also defended the concept of Spinoza. Yet none of them could ignore what Jacobi summed up in his concept, by considering the ultimate defect and disastrous dead-end of modern thought in a dramatic expressive term called Nihilism. 

### Core Principles & Key Characteristics
Nihilism is often associated with extreme pessimism and radical skepticism that questions or rejects common existential values.
While the mainstream frequently reduces it to mere destructiveness, different forms of nihilism are discussed in more detail later on.
Rather than only pointing to collapse, nihilism can also prompt individuals to confront the absence of fixed foundations and shape
their own boundaries, perspectives, and lived experiences.

Although different variants of nihilism focus on different apsects, the following principles are core characteristics of nihilism.

- **No inherent purpose or objective value:** Nihilism argues that there is no built-in purpose or objective value structure, since everything in 
existence, meaning or framework, is merely shaped through human perspective and interpretation.

- **Rejection of absolute truth:** Without a fixed transcendent framework which claims to know the absolute, universal truth, every objective moral authority is called into question.

- **Absence of intrinsic meaning:** Active nihilism argues that meaning is not inherent in things themselves. It forces a confrontation with emptiness or the task of 
self-authored value. 

### Worldview & View of Humanity 
In a nihilistic worldview, reality is nowhere anchored, whether in a metaphysical order, transcendent purpose, or inherent moral structure.
This perspective echoes the pre-Socratic philosopher Heraclitus, who said that the world is based on the principle of dynamic change.
This constant change of nature and humanity is often called 'flux' or 'stream' of life and is contrary to a static, predefined worldview.
While Schoppenhauer associated this flux primarily with disappointments and despair, Nietzsche instead views it as a dynamic tension and source of creative challenges and opportunities. In its most radical and extreme form, this worldview also attempts to negate the belief in any inherent natural order or 'moral structure'. It is a denial of the world´s intrinsic value, and not merely the intrinsic values of the particular objects, events or experiencing entities in this world.   

In Nietzsche´s perspective of nihilism, human nature mirrors Nature itself: both are, as noted earlier, in constant transition and a process of self-diversification. 
Active nihilism (or creative individuality) rejects fixed, conventional, traditional or transcendent frameworks and values, so that individuals can form their own values and perspectives. One quote captures this tension clearly: 

*"We are not creatures of infinite power and aptitude, and yet we have the creative capacity to transcend particular limitations within those inescapable conditions that have been established by Nature."*

Out of the perspective of nihilism, group-oriented and traditional-bound individuals accept the externally imposed duties without questioning them. Whereas passive nihilists are individuals who break away from conventional boundaries, but since they lack the self-knowledge and creative drive of active nihilism, they remain in 'negative freedom' and eventually slide toward life-negation and self-denial.
